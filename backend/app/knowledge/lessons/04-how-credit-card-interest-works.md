## APR is a comparison measure

APR stands for annual percentage rate. It is a way of expressing the yearly cost of borrowing, including interest and certain charges, so that credit products can be compared. The figure shown for a card is not always the exact rate that applies to every person, transaction or part of a balance. Check your agreement and statements for the terms that apply to your account.

Interest is the cost of borrowing when you do not clear the relevant balance within the time allowed by your card terms. The way it is worked out can depend on the card, the type of transaction and when payments are made. Purchases, cash withdrawals and balance transfers may be treated differently. A promotional offer can also have conditions and an end date, so check what happens when it finishes.

If you clear the statement balance in full by the due date, you may avoid interest on purchases under the card's terms. This is not a universal rule for every transaction: cash withdrawals or other activity can have different treatment. Your card agreement is the place to check the details.

## Why a balance can fall slowly

A card payment does not necessarily reduce the amount borrowed by the same amount. Some of the payment may cover interest, fees or other charges first, depending on the agreement. The rest reduces the balance. If new spending is added, the amount owed can grow even while you are making payments.

Minimum payments are designed to meet a minimum contractual amount, not necessarily to clear the debt quickly. When the balance falls, the minimum amount may also change. If you pay only the minimum and continue using the card, repayment can take a long time and the total cost can increase. The exact outcome depends on the balance, rate, payment rules, fees and future spending.

## Understand your own statement

Look for the statement balance, minimum payment, payment date, interest charged and any separate transaction balances. If something is unclear, ask the card provider how payments are allocated and what would happen if you paid more than the minimum. Avoid missing a due date while trying to work out a longer-term plan.

If you can afford to pay more, you could compare options using your actual statement information. Leave enough room for essentials and avoid making a plan that depends on money you may not have. If payments are becoming difficult, contact the provider early and seek free guidance from MoneyHelper or a debt advice charity such as StepChange.
